## What is a business cycle?

A **business cycle** is the rise and fall of economic activity around its
long-run trend. Despite the name, cycles are not regular: their length and
strength are hard to predict.

## The phases

- **Expansion:** output and employment rise
- **Peak:** activity reaches its highest point before turning down
- **Recession (contraction):** output and employment fall
- **Trough:** activity reaches its lowest point before recovering

A common rule of thumb calls two quarters in a row of falling real GDP a
recession. Some countries use broader measures. A very deep and long recession
is often called a **depression**.

## The output gap

**Potential output** is what the economy can produce when its resources are used
at normal rates. The **output gap** compares actual output with potential:

**Output gap = ((actual output − potential output) ÷ potential output) × 100**

### Example

Potential output is 500 and actual output is 480.

Output gap = ((480 − 500) ÷ 500) × 100 = **−4%**

A negative gap usually means unemployment is above its natural rate. A positive
gap means the economy is running above its normal level, which often pushes
inflation up.

## What moves with the cycle?

- **Procyclical:** moves in the same direction as output, such as consumption, investment, and employment
- **Countercyclical:** moves in the opposite direction, such as the unemployment rate

Investment usually swings much more than consumption. Firms can delay buying
machines, while households still need food and housing.

Economists also compare the timing of indicators:

- **Leading:** tends to change before the economy, such as new orders or building permits
- **Coincident:** tends to change with the economy, such as industrial production
- **Lagging:** tends to change after the economy, such as the unemployment rate

## Why do cycles happen?

- **Demand shocks:** sudden changes in spending, such as a fall in confidence or exports
- **Supply shocks:** sudden changes in production or costs, such as a drought or an oil price jump
- **Financial crises:** banks lend less, so households and firms spend less
- **Policy changes:** changes in taxes, government spending, or interest rates

Shocks can spread. When one firm cuts jobs, its workers spend less, so other
firms sell less too.

Economists disagree about which causes matter most. One common explanation is
that many prices and wages adjust slowly, so output and employment change first.
