## Why do countries trade?

Trade lets countries specialize and consume more than they could alone.

- **Absolute advantage:** producing more with the same resources
- **Comparative advantage:** producing at a lower **opportunity cost**, meaning giving up less of something else

Comparative advantage is what makes trade worthwhile. Even a country that is
better at everything can gain from trade.

### Example

In one day, a worker can produce:

- **Country A:** 6 shirts or 3 bags of rice
- **Country B:** 2 shirts or 2 bags of rice

Opportunity cost of one bag of rice:

1. Country A: 6 ÷ 3 = 2 shirts
2. Country B: 2 ÷ 2 = 1 shirt

Country A has an absolute advantage in both goods. But Country B has the
**comparative advantage in rice**, and Country A has it in shirts. Both can gain
if one bag of rice trades for between 1 and 2 shirts.

## Winners and losers

Trade can raise a country's total income, but not everyone gains. Consumers and
exporters usually benefit, while firms and workers that compete with imports may
lose.

Governments sometimes limit trade:

- **Tariff:** a tax on imports
- **Quota:** a limit on the quantity of imports

In the standard model of a small country, these raise domestic prices and help
local producers, but the losses to the country are larger than the gains.

## Recording trade: the balance of payments

- **Current account:** trade in goods and services, plus income and transfers from abroad
- **Capital and financial account:** mainly purchases and sales of assets, such as shares, bonds, and factories

**Net exports = exports − imports**

A country with a current account deficit spends more abroad than it earns there.
It must borrow from other countries or sell assets to them. A deficit is not
always bad: it depends on how the borrowed funds are used.

## Exchange rates

The **nominal exchange rate** is the price of one currency in terms of another.

- **Appreciation:** a currency becomes stronger
- **Depreciation:** a currency becomes weaker

### Example

Suppose the rate moves from 15,000 to 16,000 rupiah per US dollar. The rupiah has
**depreciated**, because one dollar now costs more rupiah.

- An Indonesian product priced at 150,000 rupiah falls from 10 dollars to about 9.38 dollars, so exports become cheaper for foreigners.
- A 10-dollar import rises from 150,000 to 160,000 rupiah, so imports become more expensive.

Net exports therefore tend to rise after a depreciation, although this can take
time. More expensive imports can also add to inflation.

The **real exchange rate** also takes price levels into account. It compares the
cost of goods in two countries, so it matters most for trade.

## Capital flows

Savers look for good returns. If interest rates in a country rise compared with
other countries, foreign funds tend to flow in and the currency tends to
appreciate, other things equal.

Exchange rates can be **floating** (set mainly by markets), **fixed** (held by the
central bank), or somewhere in between. A country cannot have a fixed exchange
rate, free capital flows, and an independent monetary policy all at once.
