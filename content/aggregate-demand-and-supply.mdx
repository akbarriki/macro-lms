## The big picture

The **AD–AS model** explains two things at once: the economy's real output
(real GDP) and its overall price level. Think of a graph with real output on
the horizontal axis and the price level on the vertical axis.

## Aggregate demand

**Aggregate demand (AD)** is the total amount of goods and services that buyers
want at each price level:

**AD = C + I + G + NX**

The AD curve slopes downward for three reasons. When the price level falls:

- **Wealth effect:** money buys more, so people feel richer and consume more.
- **Interest rate effect:** people need less money for purchases, interest rates fall, and investment rises.
- **Exchange rate effect:** lower interest rates weaken the currency, so net exports rise.

AD **shifts right** when spending rises at every price level, for example because
of higher confidence, tax cuts, more government purchases, or stronger exports.
It **shifts left** when the opposite happens.

## Aggregate supply

**Long-run aggregate supply (LRAS)** is vertical at potential output. In the
long run, output depends on labor, capital, natural resources, and technology,
not on the price level.

**Short-run aggregate supply (SRAS)** slopes upward. Many wages and prices adjust
slowly, so when the price level is higher than expected, firms earn more and
produce more.

SRAS **shifts left** when production costs or the expected price level rise. It
**shifts right** when they fall.

## Equilibrium

- **Short run:** output and the price level are where AD meets SRAS.
- **Long run:** AD, SRAS, and LRAS all meet, so output equals potential output.

## When the curves shift

### A fall in demand

Confidence drops and AD shifts left.

1. **Short run:** output falls and the price level falls.
2. **Long run:** expected prices and wages fall, so SRAS shifts right. Output returns to potential, at a lower price level.

### A rise in costs

Oil prices jump and SRAS shifts left.

1. **Short run:** output falls and the price level rises. This mix is called **stagflation**.
2. **Long run:** if AD does not change and the cost increase does not affect potential output, wages eventually fall and SRAS shifts back.

### The policy dilemma

After a fall in demand, raising AD can help both output and the price level
return to where they were. After a rise in costs there is a trade-off: raising AD
helps output but pushes prices even higher.

In real economies, inflation is usually positive. A lower price level in this
model often shows up as slower inflation than would otherwise have happened.
