## What is fiscal policy?

**Fiscal policy** is the government's use of spending and taxes to influence the
economy.

- **Expansionary:** more spending or lower taxes, which raises aggregate demand
- **Contractionary:** less spending or higher taxes, which lowers aggregate demand

Expansionary policy is usually used in a recession. Contractionary policy can
help when the economy is overheating and inflation is high.

## The multiplier

When the government buys something, someone earns income and spends part of it,
which becomes income for someone else. This is the **multiplier effect**.

The **marginal propensity to consume (MPC)** is the share of each extra unit of
income that people spend. In the simplest model:

**Spending multiplier = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC)**

**Tax multiplier = −MPC ÷ (1 − MPC)**

### Example

Suppose MPC is 0.75.

1. Spending multiplier = 1 ÷ (1 − 0.75) = 4
2. Government purchases rise by 10, so output can rise by up to 10 × 4 = **40**
3. Tax multiplier = −0.75 ÷ 0.25 = −3, so a tax cut of 10 can raise output by up to **30**

The tax cut has a smaller effect because people save part of it. Real-world
multipliers are usually smaller than this simple model suggests, because of
taxes, imports, and changes in interest rates and prices.

## Crowding out

When the government borrows more, interest rates may rise. Higher rates can
reduce private investment. This is called **crowding out**. It tends to be
smaller when the economy is weak.

## Automatic stabilizers

Some policies help without any new decision:

- **Income taxes** fall when incomes fall.
- **Unemployment benefits**, where they exist, rise when more people lose jobs.

These **automatic stabilizers** support spending in a recession and slow it in a boom.

## Deficits and debt

**Budget balance = government revenue − government spending**

Here, spending includes transfers and interest payments.

- A **deficit** means spending is greater than revenue in one year.
- **Government debt** is the total the government owes, built up from past borrowing.

A deficit is like water flowing into a bathtub; debt is the water already in it.
Economists often compare debt with GDP to judge how easily it can be managed.

## When does fiscal policy work well?

It helps most when the economy has unused resources and the policy is quick and
well targeted. It is harder to use because of time lags:

- **Recognition lag:** noticing the problem
- **Decision lag:** agreeing on a policy
- **Impact lag:** waiting for it to affect the economy

If a policy arrives too late, it can make the cycle worse.
